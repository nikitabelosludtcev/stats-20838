Week 6 · logistic regression

Logit: a probability, an odds, and exp(coefficient)

You cannot add a logit coefficient to a probability the way you add euros. First turn it into a multiplier of the odds.

Logit is what you use when Y is 1 or 0: a high sale or not, bought Red Bull or not, left or not. An ordinary OLS line can print a “probability” of 1.4 or of −0.2. Logit keeps the forecast between 0 and 1.

Inside it sits a linear sum. That sum is called the log-odds, the logarithm of the odds:

log(odds) = b₀ + b₁x₁ + b₂x₂ + …
odds = P / (1 − P)
P = 1 / (1 + exp(−(b₀ + b₁x₁ + …)))

Odds are not a probability. If P = 0.25, then for one “success” there are three “failures”, and odds = 0.25 / 0.75 = 1/3. If the probability rises from 0.25 to 0.50, the odds rise from 1/3 to 1, so they triple. Saying “the probability tripled” would be wrong. It doubled.

The coefficient b in the table is an addition to the log-odds. On the exam you are almost always asked for the multiplier:

  • odds ratio = exp(b)
  • if OR > 1, the odds are higher by (OR − 1) × 100%
  • if OR < 1, the odds are lower by (1 − OR) × 100%

The phrase “42% higher” in the mock solution is computed exactly this way: OR = 1.42, (1.42 − 1) × 100 = 42. Those are percent of the odds, not percentage points of probability.

A small numerical example

The simple Advertising model, which the next page takes apart. The TV coefficient is 0.0210. OR = exp(0.0210) = 1.021. Each extra unit of TV spend multiplies the odds of a high sale by 1.021, which is about 2.1% higher.

For ten units: exp(0.0210 × 10) = exp(0.210) = 1.233. Plus 23.3% on the odds. Ten units are easier to say to a manager than “2.1% for each tiny unit”.

The probability at TV = 100 in the simple model: the linear piece is −3.9673 + 0.0210 × 100 = −1.8673. P = 1 / (1 + exp(1.8673)) ≈ 0.133. The notebook prints 0.13345. That is where “13.3%” comes from, and it is already a probability, not an odds.

The course’s main mistakeSeeing OR = 1.42 and writing “the probability of purchase rose by 42%”. The mock solution writes odds. If you convert to a probability, you must plug a particular person into the formula for P. One percent does not apply to everyone.

When a coefficient is significant

Next to the OR sit a p-value and a confidence interval. For an OR, the interval that means “no effect” contains 1, not 0. If the OR interval contains 1, the data are compatible with no link. In the simple TV model the OR interval is [1.015; 1.027]. The number 1 is not inside, and p < 0.001.

The mock comments on both 5% and 10%. They call p = 0.051 significant at 10%. p = 0.109 is not. Do not round 0.051 down to “not significant, cross it out of the answer”. In the Red Bull solution that coefficient is discussed.
