Week 3 · privacy

Privacy: only what the lecture actually covers

The topic is wide on the slides, and for an attending exam it is background. Deeper reading is pushed into separate papers for non-attending students. This page stays inside the deck.

The slide’s logic: data improve targeting and personal offers, and they create problems at the same moment. There are three actors: the customer, the firm, and the regulator.

What is shown concretely:

  • Data breaches rose sharply after 2015. The year-by-year numbers are a chart. They are not in the extracted text, so there is nothing to memorise point by point.
  • Lambrecht and Tucker (2019): women were shown STEM career ads less often than men. That is algorithmic bias, a tilt in the algorithm, not “neutral mathematics”.
  • Large fines for breaking the GDPR. The amounts are drawn on the slide and are not a clean series in the extracted text. Remember that fines exist, and the name GDPR. Do not invent a figure.
  • A survey: about 92% of users worldwide have some privacy concern. The slide marks the source as 2019.
  • The privacy paradox, from USC Annenberg. People under and over 35 are, in general, not happy to hand over personal data. They are clearly more willing when the benefit in return is clear. The slide has agreement shares around 0.70, 0.51, 0.77, and 0.40, but the labels on the bars are scrambled in the PDF text. For the exam, the idea of the paradox is safer than memorising those four numbers.
  • Apple’s App Tracking Transparency: the phone asks permission for an app to track you. The slide’s slogan: Privacy. That’s iPhone.
  • Amazon pays people for data through the panel at panel.amazon.com. Personal information starts to have a price.
An honest boundaryThe closing class adds, for non-attending students, Dubé et al. (2025) on the consequences of privacy regulation, and the Madrigal case. Those texts are not in the uploaded folder. If you are non-attending, the slides are not enough. If you are attending, do not spend a week guessing at a paper you do not have. Go back to the field test and to reading coefficients.

A short answer, already worked

Question. “Customers say they do not want to share data, but they leave an email for a discount. What does the lecture call this, and what follows for the firm?”

  1. The name on the slide: the privacy paradox.
  2. The content: a refusal in an abstract survey, and consent when the benefit is clear, live side by side.
  3. The consequence the lecture actually draws: asking for data can be part of the offer, not only hidden tracking. Next to that sit the risks: the regulator, a fine, a biased algorithm, and lost trust.
  4. What not to answer: an exact fine percentage, or the exact height of a bar, if you are not reading it off the figure.
