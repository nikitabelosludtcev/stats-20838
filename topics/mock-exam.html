Part 1. Red Bull

The brand is watching the wellness trend. There are secondary data on people who bought in the energy-drink category. A random sample of 500 people: which brand they took, how much they paid, whether they had taken a “healthy” energy drink such as Celsius before, whether the purchase was on promo, plus a portrait of the customer.

The model is below. Y: Red Bull = 1, another brand = 0, among people who bought in the category in the last 6 months.

The baseline of the age dummies is customers older than 55: there is no column for them. The baseline of gender is not male.

1.1 Which model was estimated, and why?

1.2 Read the first substantive blocks: promo, price, healthy, age, gender. What is significant? By how much do the odds of choosing Red Bull change?

1.3 How might you explain price? Should you advise a price increase, since it “does no harm”?

1.4 Who should receive the coupon, and why?

1.5 Red Bull wants a Simply Organic line and wants to test willingness to pay and the probability of purchase among people who have already taken a healthy energy drink. What approach, and which steps?

Solution to part 1

1.1 Logistic regression. Y is a qualitative 0/1 variable, and the table has an odds-ratio column, Exp(Coef.).

1.2 Promo is significant at 5% (p = 0.038): for people who bought on promo, the odds of choosing Red Bull are 42% higher, (1.42 − 1)×100. Price is not significant (0.109). Healthy is significant at 5%: the odds are about 39% lower, 1 − 0.61. Age 36–55 is significant at 5%: the odds are 20% higher relative to people older than 55. Age 26–35 is significant at 10% (p = 0.071): about 8% higher. Under 25 does not differ from the over-55 baseline (p = 0.956). Male is significant at 10% (p = 0.051): the odds are about 34% higher. Everywhere this is odds, not “the probability rose by that many percent”.

1.3 There is no single correct explanation. A careful criticism is acceptable: little price variation in the data, strong loyalty, brand reputation, or other reasons why price in this category is barely noticed. You must not advise a price increase from an insignificant coefficient alone. Insignificance does not prove the absence of harm. This is also not an experiment with price.

1.4 First, people under 25 and men: the interactions Promo × Age < 25 (p = 0.033) and Promo × Male (p = 0.024) are positive, so the link between the promo and the odds of choice is stronger. A lower priority for people who have already taken a healthy drink: the interaction is negative, p = 0.067. The cell Exp = 1.49 next to the coefficient 0.17 does not add up. exp(0.17) ≈ 1.19. Do not lean on 49%.

1.5 A random experiment. For example a field test in which people who previously took a healthy drink are randomly shown different prices of the new line, and real purchases are observed. The steps they expect: whom you include, which price conditions, how you assign at random, which outcome you measure, how you compare purchase shares. Willingness to pay comes out of this only indirectly. There is no single canonical design. There is a requirement on the logic.

Part 2. Green Product Launch

A sportswear brand wants to know whether an explicit sustainability sentence raises engagement with an Instagram post: likes, comments, shares. A new line of running shoes made from recycled materials. The brand had already talked about sustainability, including billboards in Milan.

A field test in eight cities. Group A: followers in Milan, Venice, Florence, and Palermo, shown an ordinary product post. Group B: Turin, Bologna, Genoa, and Catania, the same visual plus an explicit sustainability sentence. The cities were split as “comparable in size”, not by random assignment of a person.

2.1 Name the dependent and independent variables. Comment on the design and its limits.

2.2 The regression is below. Read the coefficients and give a recommendation.

VariableCoef.p-value
Intercept (b₀)1200.000
Sustainability Message (b₁)580.004

2.3 Besides the shoes, the brand sells clothing and accessories. Early Access: some customers could buy the shoes a week before the general launch, and the others only after. Three months later, Early Access participants had spent €58 on average on clothing and accessories, and the others €13. The mean includes zeros for people who bought nothing. How would you estimate the effect of the programme, and which data do you need?

Solution to part 2

2.1 Y is engagement, the sum of likes, comments, and shares. X is a dummy for the sustainability sentence: 1 if the sentence was there. Limits from the solution: there is no random assignment, and the cities could have differed on their own. Milan had already seen the billboards, and the solution also mentions the Design Week context as a previous touch. City audiences differ in familiarity with the brand and in interest in the environment. Different hashtags such as #SustainableRunning could themselves have attracted a different audience and mixed that into the effect of the text.

2.2 120 is the expected engagement without the sentence. +58 means the sentence is associated with 58 more interactions. p = 0.004, so the link is significant. It is not yet a cause: the design did not remove outside differences. The direction of bias from the Milan billboards is uncertain. A cautious recommendation: the signal is positive, and you cannot scale it as a proven effect of the text until there is random assignment.

2.3 The observed gap is €58 − €13 = €45. People signed themselves into Early Access. They may already have been more loyal. That is selection bias, not the effect of the programme. The working path in the solution is propensity score matching. You need data from before the programme: past spending, past engagement, participation in past promotions, an indicator of Early Access, and spending on clothing and accessories in the three months after, including the zeros.

ItemIn your answer?
