Week 3 · the jargon of an experiment

Groups, bias, and the six design steps

These words then stand in every case: control, treatment, selection bias, confounding. Learn them before Artea, not in the middle of a table.

WordThe slide’s definition, in plain English
Independent variable, XWhat the researcher changes. Example: the colour of the pack
ManipulationThe act “we changed X”, not merely noticing that different people have a different X
Dependent variable, O on the slideWhat should move after X. Example: brand choice. You judge success by O
Experimental groupThe people who received the treatment
Control groupThe people who did not. You compare against them
ConfoundingSomething other than X that also moves O. The slide’s example: competitors’ reactions
Selection biasThe groups already differ, at the start, on something important. Then a gap in O may come from the people, not from X
RandomizationAssignment to groups by chance. The main way not to gather “special” people into the treatment
Treatment effectThe result of the comparison, for example 0.60 − 0.40 = 0.20

Connecticut and Florida flicker on the slide’s picture. That is a hint at a classic trap: two states are bad “groups” if they were not assigned at random. The residents already differ. You do not need to memorise the state names. You need the moral: geography as a way of splitting people often drags selection bias with it. That is exactly what appears in the mock, in the case about Italian cities.

The six design steps

Experimental design is a set of procedures. The slide numbers six questions.

  1. What do you want to change or control? For example, the pack.
  2. What levels will X take? For example, two colours.
  3. What is O, the dependent outcome? For example, the choice.
  4. Whom do you include, and how do you select them?
  5. How will you avoid selection bias? Usually, random assignment.
  6. How will you reduce outside factors? Competitors, the season, another channel, a different text.

A worked answer in exam form

A shop shows a new banner only to visitors from Milan, and the old banner only to visitors from Turin. Purchases are higher in Milan. Can you say the banner is the cause?

  1. X looks like the banner, and O is the purchase. The order in time is fine if the banner came before the purchase.
  2. There is a link: the cities differ.
  3. Another explanation is very plausible: the cities differ in income, in habit with the brand, in weather, in other advertising. There was no random assignment.
  4. This is selection bias. The gap in means cannot be called a treatment effect of the banner.
  5. What to do, using the six steps: choose the banner’s levels, the outcome, assign the banner at random inside the same stream of people, and hold the other campaigns the same.
A sentence that earns marks“The groups are similar in size” does not cure bias. In the mock the city groups are also comparable in size, and the solution still puts the lack of randomization first.
