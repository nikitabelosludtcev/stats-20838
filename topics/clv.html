Week 5 · Customer Development

What it is worth to keep a customer

CLV turns “let them stay” into money. A small shift in churn can be worth more than it looks, because the link is convex.

After the first purchase the firm asks the slide’s question: and now what? Acquisition cost money. If the person stays, that money is earned back. In mobile telecom the slide quotes retention of 70–85% a year and industry losses of up to $10 billion. That is a picture of scale, not a number to plug into a case.

Customer development is the set of actions that create value for the customer and for the firm. Customer retention is so that they do not leave. On the slide, satisfaction is tied to margin: in the example, very satisfied customers bring $620 of profit each, and very dissatisfied customers bring minus $80. The table’s source is Rust, Zeithaml and Lemon, adapted. Do not use those $620 inside Red Bull. The conclusion you need: satisfied customers bring more, and acquiring a new one is, on average, more expensive than keeping a current one. The slide attributes that last point to TARP research.

The formula you must be able to plug numbers into
CLV = m × (1 + δ) / (δ + c) = m × (1 + δ) / (1 + δ − r)
m is margin per period, r is the retention rate, c = 1 − r is the churn rate, and δ (sometimes written d) is the discount rate.

Why (1+δ) on top and δ+c on the bottom. The slide derives it from an infinite geometric series: money today, then again next period with the probability of still being there, and every future euro is worth less because of discounting. On the exam you are more often asked to plug numbers in than to re-derive the series. The series is worth recognising. Expected length of life, if churn is constant:

expected lifetime = 1 / c

The slide’s table: churn 28% → 1/0.28 ≈ 3.6 years; 20% → 5.0 years; 15% → 6.7 years. That is not CLV. That is a length of time. Money appears when you join the length to the margin and the discount.

The worked example on the slide

m = $500, δ = 0.14. Compare churn of 20% and 15%.

  1. At c = 0.20: CLV = 500 × 1.14 / (0.14 + 0.20) = 570 / 0.34 = $1,676.
  2. At c = 0.15: CLV = 570 / (0.14 + 0.15) = 570 / 0.29 = $1,965.
  3. The gap for one customer: 1965 − 1676 = $289.
  4. On a base of 5,000,000 customers the slide gets on the order of $1.45 billion of extra profit. 289 × 5,000,000 = 1.445 billion. The arithmetic matches the slide’s caption.

The chart is called convex: the lower the churn, the steeper CLV rises. Cutting churn from 20% to 15% is worth more money than the same five-point gap somewhere at a very high churn. So “minus five points” cannot be judged by eye.

You can raise CLV with three handles on the formula: a larger m (a higher ticket or a lower cost), a smaller c (they stay longer), and more careful spending on acquisition. The slide’s diagnosis: look at CLV / AC. If you spend more on a customer than they bring, something is wrong. In Books R Us, AC = €10 is also subtracted from CLV, and the discount there is d = 0.12. On the chart, the historical CLV of a multichannel customer is €97.19, and of a single-channel customer €30.85. The m and r behind those two bars are not labelled in the slide text, so quote the bars as a case result. Do not recompute them blind.

A common missPlug retention in where churn belongs. If 80% stay, then c = 0.20, not 0.80. The denominator holds c, or (1+δ−r). Mixing them up is easy, and it immediately doubles the lifetime.
