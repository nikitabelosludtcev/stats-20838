Week 5 · choosing X

Predictors, overfitting, and false links

A good X is a measurable hypothesis about behaviour, not any column that happens to correlate.

The slide suggests turning a hypothesis into a variable. For churn from a gym that might be: how the person attends, who they are, how you spoke to them, and whether they replied. The letters X₁ … Xₚ are simply the list of those measurements.

How the set is chosen. The slide cites Neslin et al. (2006): academics and managers often start with EDA, exploratory data analysis, that is, looking at the data. Then a search over subsets is possible. Take the set with the highest R², or with the lowest AIC or BIC.

Three indexes, in words
AIC = −2·LL + 2k. The penalty for the number of parameters k grows slowly.
BIC = −2·LL + ln(N)·k. The penalty grows with the sample size, so extra X’s are punished harder.
Pseudo R² = 1 − LL_full / LL₀. This is not a share of variance. For ordinary OLS, R² is the share of explained variance. For logit, the word pseudo is there on purpose.

LL is the log-likelihood, a number for “how plausible the data are at these coefficients”. LL₀ is the same thing for a model that has only an intercept, no X. Smaller AIC and BIC are better, when you compare models on the same data. Higher R² is a tighter fit, but not automatically a better forecast.

A spurious correlation, and two failures

The slide points at Tyler Vigen’s charts: series that move together by pure coincidence. The lecturer’s sentence: predictors can correlate by chance, so first check that the link has a meaning, and do not throw every column at the wall.

  • Overfitting. Too many X’s. The model memorises the noise of the old sample and fails on a new one.
  • Underfitting. Too few X’s. The model misses a stable signal that is in the data.

The remedy for overfitting is one you already know: validation on people who were not used when the X’s were chosen. If you chose the X’s by searching hundreds of columns on the whole base, the “check” sample is no longer clean. The slide does not formalise that subtlety. It is enough to say: both the choice of X and the coefficients are checked out of sample.

A short worked answer

An analyst added 40 features. In-sample pseudo R² rose from 0.06 to 0.40. Out-sample lift of the top decile fell from 2.5 to 1.1. What happened?

  1. A rise in pseudo R² on the training people is not a victory by itself. A fit index rises when the model is given more flexibility.
  2. A fall in lift on new people is the symptom of overfitting.
  3. The action: drop features with no managerial meaning, compare AIC and BIC, and keep the set that holds its lift on the outside.

On the closing class, an example logit has a pseudo R² of only 0.056, and the model is still interpreted through its significant coefficients. A small pseudo R² does not forbid reading a single coefficient. It forbids the sentence “the model explained 5.6% of behaviour” as if that were an OLS R².
