Week 5 · the lift chart

Checking a forecast on new people

A model that remembers old customers perfectly and confuses new ones is useless for targeting. So you cut the sample.

Presentation. Customer Development. Calibration, validation, and the lift chart. The closing class repeats the same point with the words “out-sample”.

Two samples

The calibration sample, also called in-sample: this is where the coefficients are estimated. The validation sample, out-sample or holdout: the coefficients are not fitted here. You only predict Y and see whether you were right.

The teaching picture: N = 100 in total, 75 inside, 25 outside. The slide says that, when the size is small, any sensible share for calibration will do. How to cut: at random, not “the most convenient people into calibration, the rest into the check”.

The slide’s picture also has an ordinary regression on 75 observations: one X, coefficient 1.605, R² = 0.716. That is an illustration of a stats output, not a case to memorise. Its role is to show that coefficients are obtained in-sample.

The lift chart

After the forecast, people are sorted by predicted Y and cut into groups: halves, quintiles, deciles. A decile is a tenth. Decile 1 on the churn slides is the riskiest top, if that is the agreement. In Pilgrim, decile 1 is also the highest predicted risk.

Lift of group k = the group’s average actual response / the average response of the whole sample

If the lift of the top decile equals 3, that tenth leaves three times as often as the average customer. The slide writes the example: Lift of the top decile = 3. On the picture the bars run roughly 0.69, 0.43, 0.09, and then zeros. A good churn forecast looks like a staircase going down: the first group really is worse than the second.

The targeting rule on the slide: take groups where lift > 1, that is, worse than average, if you are talking about risk. The letter on the slide is λₖ > 1.

How the closing class reads lift

There are two out-sample charts, and they are a ready-made comment.

  • Lift for a 0/1 purchase: a modest forecast. The bars do not fall cleanly downward. The first decile is about 60% purchases, but the gap after that is weak. The model separates “very likely” buyers poorly.
  • Lift for profit: a strong forecast. From decile 1 to decile 10, average profit falls. The first decile is about 7,000, clearly above the tail. You can already aim that model at valuable people.

The comment that takes the mark is not “the chart is pretty”. It is “is there a monotone decline, and how far does the first decile pull away from the average?”.

A common missPraising a model for its in-sample lift. A fit on the same people the coefficients were learned from almost always looks better. The closing class writes OUT-SAMPLE on purpose. Ask yourself: are these new people?

A worked example

Average churn in the base is 10%. In the top decile, actual churn is 30%. Lift = 30 / 10 = 3. If a contact is expensive, starting with that decile is rational for a risk forecast. It does not yet mean a gift will keep them. Retention needs a test, not a lift chart.

Practice

Average response 10%, and 30% in the top group. What is the lift?
