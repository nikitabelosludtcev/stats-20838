Week 7 · matching

When the test can no longer be run

If the treatment was already handed out non-randomly, comparing recipients with “everyone else” is dangerous. Matching looks among the others for people who are similar.

The slide’s problem: a marketing action often reaches people who are not random. Recipients differ from the rest. A gap in sales may be the people, not the promotion. The lecturer’s sentence: you cannot tell whether it was the treatment or the composition of the group.

The idea: in the wider base there are people who look like the recipients but did not receive the promotion. Pair them. This is not a real experiment. It is an attempt to build a plausible comparison out of the archive.

What is missing

Each person has two potential outcomes: Y(1), if the promotion had been given, and Y(0), if it had not. The data show only one of them. The other is called the counterfactual, “how it would otherwise have been”. Randomization makes the groups interchangeable. Matching tries to replace it with similarity on the observed X’s.

The slide names other methods, without instructions: difference-in-differences (compare changes over time) and regression discontinuity (compare people who landed just on opposite sides of a threshold). Their procedures are not in the deck. On the exam it is enough to recognise the name and to say that the tool this lecture works through is matching, in particular the propensity score.

Who to pair with whom

  • Exact match. The same values of the chosen features. The slide has an exercise “who fits customer A and customer B” on past orders. When there are many features, an exact twin is often missing.
  • Nearest neighbor. The closest person who did not receive the promotion, even if they are not a twin.
  • Propensity score. It compresses many features into one number: the probability of receiving the treatment given the observed X’s before the promotion. Similar probabilities are treated as “similar people”.

Where that probability comes from. The slide asks a question you “should already know”: the outcome is binary, received the action or not, and there are many features. That is a logit (or a probit). Here the model’s Y is not sales. It is the fact of treatment itself. Sales are compared afterwards, inside the pairs.

propensity score = P(treatment = 1 | X before the promotion)

When you may believe the conclusion, cautiously

Three conditions from the slide.

  1. Observed confounders. We measured the important reasons why the promotion was given and why sales later differed. Matching does not cure everything unobserved.
  2. Overlap. Recipients and the others share some values of the score. If someone has probability 0.99 and everyone else tops out at 0.2, there is no pair.
  3. Balance after the pairing. Features on which the groups differed should move closer inside the pairs. On the Sample Box figure they look at the absolute standardized mean difference and are pleased when, after matching, every shown feature is below 0.10.

Put into X only what was known before the promotion: past purchases, tenure, the channel. Do not put the outcome after the promotion into the matching. Otherwise you are pairing on the very thing you want to explain.

The effect computed on the people who received the promotion is called the ATT, the average treatment effect on the treated: the average effect for those who actually received it. It is not necessarily the effect for a random customer from the whole base.

How this differs from ArteaIn Artea the coupon was assigned at random, so matching is not needed. The gap between the groups is enough. Matching appears when there was no randomness: Sample Box, and, in the mock, the Early Access programme that people signed themselves into.
